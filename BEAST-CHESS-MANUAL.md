## 1.1 The 6-Question Blunder Firewall ⭐ (the most valuable page in this manual)

Run this before **every single move**. Even the obvious ones — *especially* the obvious ones. In the dashboard: Playbook → “The 6-Question Blunder Firewall”.

1. **What did his last move attack or threaten?** (Look at the piece he moved and the pieces behind it.)
2. **Is anything of mine hanging right now?** Count his attackers vs my defenders on every piece and pawn.
3. **Can I give a check — and does it actually help?** (Checks are not automatically good.)
4. **Can I capture something — and is it really free?** What recaptures, and what happens after that?
5. **If I play my intended move, what is HIS best reply?** Play his move in your head first.
6. **After my move: is my king safe, and is every piece defended?**

**Why this works:** your opponent is not beating you with deep plans — he is waiting for you to move a piece to a square where it can be taken, or to ignore his threat for one move. This checklist alone is worth roughly 300 rating points at district level.

**The three-move reflex (use when short of time):** *Checks → Captures → Threats.* Look at forcing moves first, in that order. Strong players think in this order automatically.

## 1.2 The Four Opening Golden Rules

You do not need opening theory at district level. You need these four rules executed ruthlessly:

1. **Take the center with pawns** — e4/d4 as White; e5/d5 as Black. The center is the high ground: pieces there control more squares.
2. **Develop knights and bishops toward the center, one new piece per move.** Never move the same piece twice in the opening. Do not bring the queen out early — she becomes a target and you lose time.
3. **Castle by move 6–10.** King safety beats every material temptation. A king in the center loses games.
4. **Connect your rooks** and put them on open files by move 12–15.

**The pawn-move rule:** in the opening, move pawns only to (a) take the center, (b) defend the center, or (c) kick a strong piece. Every other pawn move creates a permanent weakness.

**Punish your opponent's violations:** if he brings the queen out early, develop with tempo (attack her with each developing move). If he does not castle, open the center and attack. If he moves pawns in front of his king, aim your pieces at the holes.

## 1.3 Material Values & The Counting Rule

| Piece | Value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pawn | 1 | Worth 2–4 in rook endgames (a passed pawn is gold) |
| Knight | 3 | Best in closed positions; “a knight on the rim is dim” |
| Bishop | 3.25 | Best in open positions; the bishop pair ≈ +0.5 |
| Rook | 5 | Worth more on open files; the exchange (R for minor) ≈ 1.5–2 |
| Queen | 9 | Powerful, but a target if exposed early |
| King | ∞ | A fighting piece in the endgame — activate it |

**The Counting Rule (how to never lose a piece again).** Before any capture: count **how many of my pieces attack that square** vs **how many of his defend it**. Then play the whole sequence in your head to the last recapture. If the same number attack as defend, the exchange must win material or clearly improve your position — otherwise do not start it. This single habit prevents most beginner blunders.

**Trade values to remember:** two minor pieces (6) usually beat a rook + pawn (6) in the middlegame. A rook + two pawns vs two minors is approximately equal. Never give up the bishop pair for nothing.

# PART II — THE TACTICS ARSENAL (Day 3 — the money)

Tactics are not magic. They are **eight shapes** your eyes learn to see. Once you can see them, you will win material in almost every game — and then chess becomes easy. Train them in the dashboard’s Puzzle Trainer until you can name each motif in under 3 seconds.

### 1. The FORK / Double Attack
One piece attacks two (or more) units; the opponent can only save one.
- **Knights are the fork kings** — check any enemy pieces sitting 2+1 squares apart from a single landing square.
- **Royal fork:** a knight check that also hits the queen or a rook — the #1 tactic that decides club games.
- Pawns fork constantly: two pieces on adjacent files, one rank apart, are targets for a pawn push.
- **Defence:** after every enemy knight move, ask “what is now forked?”. Never fork into a square where the forking piece is simply captured.

### 2. The PIN
A piece cannot move because something more valuable sits behind it. Absolute pin = the king is behind it (literally frozen). Relative pin = a queen/rook is behind it.
- **The killer insight: a pinned piece is not defending.** So anything it “guards” is loose. Put a bishop on g5 pinning the f6 knight to the queen — then take everything that knight was defending.
- **Pile up with a pawn** (c3/d4/e5 style) — pawns attack for free and cannot be chased away by pieces.
- **Break a pin** only with a purpose: a counter-check, a mate threat, or a capture that wins material. Otherwise add a defender or challenge the pinning piece.

### 3. The SKEWER
A reverse pin: attack the big piece (usually the king) which must move — and take the smaller piece behind it.
- Most common: check the king along a rank/file/diagonal, then capture the queen or rook behind it.
- **Never line your own king and queen up** on the same file, rank or diagonal.

### 4. DISCOVERED ATTACK & Double Check
Your piece was blocking another of your pieces. Move the front piece (with a capture or a threat) and the piece behind it attacks something huge.
- **Discovered check is the strongest version:** they MUST answer the check, so whatever your moving piece just captured is untouchable. Free material.
- **Double check** (both pieces check) → the king must move; no blocking, no capturing. Usually a mating net.

### 5. REMOVE / DEFLECT THE DEFENDER
Every attacked piece has a keeper. Eliminate it — capture it, pin it, or decoy it away — and the piece falls.
- **Overloaded defender:** one piece guarding two things cannot do both. Attack both and one falls.
- Checks and threats are the best can-openers: force the keeper to move, then take the treasure.

### 6. BACK-RANK MATE
A castled king behind three unmoved pawns can be mated on the back rank by any rook or queen that gets there.
- **Attack:** double rooks on the back rank, or sacrifice to remove the last defender (Rxe8+ then the other rook mates).
- **Defence:** make “luft” (h3/h6 or g3/g6) at a quiet moment; keep a defender on the back rank. Watch for it **every single move**.

### 7. THE IN-BETWEEN MOVE (Zwischenzug)
You expect a recapture — but instead play a check or a bigger capture first, and the whole sequence flips.
- Before every recapture, spend one second: **“is there something better first?”**
- This is where district players lose the most material: the automatic recapture that was not forced.

### 8. THE MATING PATTERNS (know them by name)
| Pattern | Shape | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Back-rank** | King trapped behind its pawns on the 8th/1st rank | Crash a rook/queen onto the back rank |
| **Smothered mate** | Knight mates a king boxed in by its own pieces | Cannot be blocked or captured |
| **Ladder mate** | Two rooks (or rook + queen) | One cuts the rank, the other checks — roll up the board |
| **Anastasia’s mate** | Knight on e7 + rook/queen on the e-file | Knight covers g8/d8-style escape squares |
| **Arabian mate** | Knight + rook in the corner | Knight covers the corner escapes, rook mates on the edge |
| **Greek gift (Bxh7+)** | Bishop sacrifice on h7 with queen + knight ready | Only when the king cannot escape to the other side |

**How to train tactics properly (this matters more than volume):**
1. Look at forcing moves first: **chest, captures, threats** in that order.
2. Before you move, list every enemy piece that is *undefended* or *pinned*.
3. When you find it, **name the motif out loud** — this is what builds the pattern library.
4. If you get it wrong, do not just read the answer — **replay the position tomorrow.** Repetition beats reading.

# PART III — THE ARMOR (Day 4 — defence & traps)

## 3.1 King Safety: the Shield and the F-Pawn Trap
- The three pawns in front of your castled king are **armour**. Every one you push is a crack.
- **Never push f3/f6 with your king castled behind it** unless you absolutely must. It opens the diagonal to your king — this is the single most common way club players get mated.
- Count **attackers vs defenders around your king** at all times. 3 attackers vs 2 defenders = danger: bring a defender, trade an attacker, or make an escape square.
- Choose your castling side according to where the enemy pawn chain points (castling into a pawn storm is suicide).
- **The Greek gift check:** before Bxh7+ style sacrifices land on you, always ask “what if he sacrifices here?” and count the escape squares.

## 3.2 Prophylaxis: ask “what does he WANT?”
5 seconds per move. It saves games.
- After every opponent move: **“his idea with that move is ___.”**
- Find his most dangerous next move and assume he will play it.
- Cover the weak colour complex (the squares your pawns do not control).
- If you cannot stop his plan, make your threats bigger — **counterplay is the best defence.**

## 3.3 The 10 Traps (learn them from BOTH sides — full interactive library in the dashboard)

| # | Trap | The bait | The antidote |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Scholar’s Mate** | Qh5 + Bc4 at f7 after …Nf6 | Defend e5 with **…Nc6 first**, then meet Qh5 with **…g6!** and only then …Nf6 |
| 2 | **Fool’s Mate** | f3 + g4 opening the e1–h4 diagonal | Never push f3/g4 (or f6/g5) with the king behind them. Develop and castle |
| 3 | **Légal’s Mate** | A “free” queen (Bxd1??) then Bxf7+ Nd5# | When you see a free queen in the opening — stop. Calculate the checks. Take the knight instead |
| 4 | **Blackburne Shilling (3…Nd4)** | Black gives the e5 pawn to lure Nxe5, then …Qg5! | Do not take the bait: **4.Nxd4 exd4 5.O-O** and you have a clean development edge |
| 5 | **Stafford Gambit** | Give …Nxe4, then Bxf2+ Ke2 Bg4# if White grabs the queen | Take the pawn but develop normally (**5.Nc3**); never take the queen while your king is on e1 with bishops raining down |
| 6 | **Fried Liver (4.Ng5)** | If Black defends with …Nxd5, Nxf7! Kxf7 Qf3+ | The cold shower is **5…Na5!** — counterattack the bishop instead of defending |
| 7 | **Damiano’s Defence (…f6)** | Black defends e5 with the f-pawn; Nxe5! fxe5 Qh5+ drags the king out | Never defend e5 with the f-pawn. Use …Nc6, …d6, or …Nf6 when safe |
| 8 | **Elephant Trap (QGD 6.Nxd5??)** | White grabs d5 while the bishop pins the queen; …Nxd5! Nxd5 Bxd8 Bb4+! | Do not capture on d5 while your bishop pins the queen. Play e3/Nf3/Bd3/O-O |
| 9 | **Englund Gambit (1.d4 e5?)** | …Qb4+ and …Qxb2; careless defence (Bd2/Bc3) gets mated on c1 | **4.Qd5!** (hits the knight and f7) or if 4.Bf4 then answer Qb4+ with **5.Qd2** (never Bd2) |
| 10 | **QGA …b5 trap** | Black holds the c4 pawn with …b5; **a4!** overloads the pawn → Qf3 wins material | If you take on c4 as Black, give the pawn back for smooth development (…e5/…Nf6/…e6) |

**The universal antidote to ALL bait:** when the position looks *too* good, stop for 60 seconds and calculate your opponent’s checks first. Nothing is free in the opening.

# PART IV — THE FINISHER (Day 5 — endgames)

## 4.1 The guaranteed mates
**King + Queen vs King** (drill it: Endgame Lab):
- The queen **pushes**, the king **mates**. First bring your king close.
- Squeeze with the queen a knight-move away from their king until it reaches the edge.
- **Stalemate trap:** when their king is in the corner, never make a quiet queen move — bring your king or give a check.
- Mate on the edge with the queen protected by your king.

**King + Rook vs King (the box method):**
- Cut the enemy king off with a rook “wall”, then shrink the box one line at a time.
- When the kings face each other (opposition), check with the rook — the king steps back and you repeat.
- Bring your king up, then mate on the edge. **Under 15 moves when drilled.**

**Two rooks (ladder mate):** one cuts the rank, the other checks — roll the king up the board.

**Two bishops:** adjacent diagonals make a net the king cannot cross; drive it to a corner, bring the king, mate.

## 4.2 King + Pawn vs King — the most valuable endgame lesson in chess
- **Key squares:** for a pawn on the 5th rank, the three squares two ranks ahead of it (e.g. pawn e5 → key squares d7, e7, f7). If your king reaches any of them, the pawn promotes — the exact position does not matter.
- **Get your king IN FRONT of the pawn.** Never push the pawn past the support of your own king.
- **Opposition:** when the kings face off with one square between them, the player *not* to move wins the fight. Use a pawn move as a waiting move to hand the move to your opponent.
- **As the defender:** get in front, block, take the opposition — a pawn ending with correct defence is a **draw**, and rook pawns (a/h) are the easiest draws of all.
- **The square rule** (when your king cannot help): draw an imaginary square from the pawn to its promotion square. Enemy king inside the square → he catches the pawn. Outside → the pawn runs home.

## 4.3 Rook endgames — the ones that decide real games
- **Tarrasch rule: put your rook BEHIND the passed pawn** — yours or his. Always, at the earliest opportunity.
- **Lucena (you have the pawn on the 7th):** build a bridge — rook to the 4th rank, walk your king out, and let the rook block the checks with its body.
- **Philidor (you are defending rook vs rook + pawn):** keep your rook on the 3rd rank blocking the pawn; when the king attacks it, swing it to the back rank and check forever from behind. **The active rook draws.**
- Never be the passive defender: activity is everything in rook endings.

## 4.4 Endgame principles (how to convert 100% of winning positions)
1. **Trade pieces, not pawns** when you are ahead in material.
2. **Activate your king immediately** — in the endgame it is a fighting piece worth about 4 points.
3. **Beware stalemate and perpetual checks** — the enemy rook checking from behind is the classic swindle. Deal with it *before* pushing pawns.
4. **Push passed pawns with support** — the rook behind, the king in front, protect it with everything.
5. **If you are worse:** aim for opposite-coloured bishops or a rook ending with an active rook. Those are the drawish structures.
6. **A win is a win.** Winning by one pawn counts the same as winning by five. Do not get greedy.

# PART V — MIDDLEGAME STRATEGY

## 5.1 What to DO after the opening (most club players have no idea — that is your edge)
Ask these questions every 3–4 moves:
- **What is my weakest piece?** Give it a job (this is what Nb1–d2–f1–g3 is in the Italian).
- **Where is his weakest point?** An isolated pawn, a backward pawn, a hole, a pinned piece — attack it with everything.
- **Which file can I own?** First rook to an open file with support is worth more than a pawn. Open the file with a pawn break.
- **Can my knight get an outpost?** A knight on a square no enemy pawn can attack (protected by your pawn) is a monster.
- **What is my pawn break?** d4/d5, e5, c5, b4 — prepare it with pieces, then execute.

## 5.2 Pawn structure — remember, pawns cannot go back
- **Isolated pawn:** a long-term weakness. If it is yours, trade or defend it; if it is his, blockade it and win it in the endgame.
- **Doubled pawns:** weak if isolated, strong if they control the center (e.g. after …exd5 in the Italian).
- **Passed pawn:** no enemy pawn in front — push it, rook behind it, king in front of it.
- **Pawn chains (French/Caro structures):** attack the **base** of the chain, not the head.
- **Colour complexes:** if you trade the bishop that matches your weak squares, you will suffer for 40 moves. Think before every bishop trade.
- **Never push pawns in front of your own king** without a concrete reason.

## 5.3 Piece quality — “find your worst piece”
- **Good bishop** = your pawns are on the opposite colour and its diagonals are open. **Bad bishop** = blocked by your own pawns. Trade your bad ones for his good ones.
- **Knights** love closed positions, outposts and central squares. They hate the rim.
- **Bishops** love open positions and long diagonals. The bishop pair is worth about half a pawn in an open middlegame.
- **Check your worst piece every 5 moves** and give it a new job. This alone wins dozens of club games.

## 5.4 How to attack the castled king — the 4-Condition Test
Do not attack unless at least 3 of these are true:
1. **More attackers than defenders** near his king (typically 3 vs 2 — count them).
2. **No faster counterattack** against your own king.
3. **A clear way in** — a weak pawn (g6/h6/h7), a pinned defender, an open file, or a diagonal to the king.
4. **Your own king is safe.**

Standard acceleration: sacrifice on h7/f7 (only with a follow-up), h-pawn push with a rook lift, knight to g5/f5, queen + bishop battery on the b1–h7 diagonal, and **trading off his best defender**. Bring the maximum number of pieces — queen and knight last.

## 5.5 When to trade — the 5 Trade Rules
1. **Ahead in material → trade pieces, not pawns.** Every pair gone makes your extra material more decisive.
2. **Behind in material → avoid trades.** Keep pieces on, create complications.
3. **Cramped → trade pieces.** Your lack of space hurts less with fewer pieces.
4. **Your bad piece for his good piece → always trade.**
5. **Attacking → trade his defenders. Defending → trade his attackers.**

# PART VI — YOUR OPENING REPERTOIRE (learn these cold)

> Every line below is **machine-verified legal** and stored in the dashboard’s Repertoire tab, with per-move notes and a quiz mode. Learn them there — this is the summary.

## 6.1 As WHITE — the Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4)
**Why:** fast development, easy plans, castle by move 6, and a nasty trap if Black is careless. It fits the Four Golden Rules perfectly.

**The main highway (Giuoco Pianissimo):**
`1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.c3 Nf6 5.d3 d6 6.O-O O-O 7.Re1 a6 8.Nbd2 Ba7 …`
- **Plan:** c3 (done) → d4 when supported → Nbd2–f1–g3 targeting f5/h5 → Re1/Re3 squeeze.
- **Remember:** your bishop on c4 stares at f7 (Black's weakest square) forever.
- **The sharper version:** `4.c3 Nf6 5.d4 exd4 6.cxd4 Bb4+ 7.Bd2 Bxd2+ 8.Nbxd2 d5` — you get the big center e4+d4 and you are better.

**Against everything else (keep it simple, take the center):**
| Black plays | You play | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Petroff** `2…Nf6` | `3.Nxe5 d6 4.Nf3 Nxe4 5.d4 d5 6.Bd3` | Take the center and castle early |
| **Sicilian** `2…c5` | **Alapin** `3.c3 d5 4.exd5 Qxd5 5.d4 Nf6 6.Nf3` | Zero theory, perfect center, queen is a target |
| **French** `2…e6` | `3.d4 d5 4.e5` (Advance) `4…c5 5.c3 Nc6 6.Nf3 Qb6 7.Bd3` | Space + a locked center; their light bishop stays bad |
| **Caro-Kann** `2…c6` | `3.d4 d5 4.e5 Bf5 5.Nf3 e6 6.Be2` | Space, then the c4 break |
| **Scandinavian** `2…d5` | `3.exd5 Qxd5 4.Nc3 Qa5 5.d4 Nf6 6.Nf3` | They waste 3 queen moves — you get free development |
| **Pirc/Modern** `2…d6/g6` | `3.d4 Nf6 4.Nc3 g6 5.Nf3 Bg7 6.Be2 O-O` | Take the whole center, then squeeze |

## 6.2 As BLACK vs 1.e4 — the Italian setup (defend with pieces, never …f6)
`1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.c3 Nf6 5.d3 d6 6.O-O O-O 7.Re1 a6 8.Bb3 Ba7`
- **Plan:** develop, castle, then hit the center with …Na5/…Bg4/…d5 at the right moment. Your bishop on a7 watches f2 and d4 forever.
- **vs 3.Bb5 (Ruy Lopez):** `…a6 4.Ba4 Nf6 5.O-O Be7 6.Re1 b5 7.Bb3 d6 8.c3 O-O` — rock solid.
- **vs 3.d4 (Scotch):** `3…exd4 4.Nxd4 Bc5 5.Be3 Qf6` — equalize immediately, centralize the queen safely.
- **vs 2.f4 (King's Gambit):** `2…exf4 3.Nf3 d5! 4.exd5 Nf6` — take the pawn, hit back at the center.
- **vs 4.Ng5 (Fried Liver attempt):** `4…d5! 5.exd5 Na5!` — the cold shower that kills 90% of junior attacks.

## 6.3 As BLACK vs 1.d4 / 1.c4 / 1.Nf3 — the universal “wall” (6 moves, one plan)
`…d5, …Nf6, …e6, …Be7, …O-O, …c5` — **the same setup against almost anything.** If you are nervous with Black, this alone saves you from losing in the opening.
- **vs the London (Bf4):** `…d5 …Nf6 …e6 …c5 …Nc6 …Bd6` then trade the bishop and take the open file ideas; target b2/g3 later.
- **vs 1.c4:** `…e6 …d5 …Nf6 …Be7 …O-O …c5` — transposes into a normal QGD, which you already know.
- Strategy: **hit their center with …c5 at the right moment**, then play on the open files.

# PART VII — MATCH DAY (Day 7 + tournament day)

## 7.1 Match-day protocol
**Before you sit down:** arrive early, water, bathroom, phone off, ten slow breaths. No last-minute study — nothing you learn in the last 30 minutes matters. Remember: *your job is not to play brilliant chess. Your job is to play clean chess and let THEM make the mistake.*

**Clock discipline:**
- **Opening:** 30 seconds total for your first 8 moves. You know the repertoire — use it.
- **Middlegame:** when a check or capture is on the board, spend 2 minutes. List 3 candidate moves, then eliminate.
- **Never drop below 3 minutes before move 30.** When low on time: play simple, safe, developing moves; avoid complications.
- **Opponent in time trouble?** Do not rush him. Keep it complicated but safe for you. Let the clock beat him.

**When you are WINNING:** trade pieces (not pawns), watch for back-rank mates and cheap swindles, activate your king, convert simply. 1–0 is not 10–0.

**When you are LOSING:** do not resign at district level — winning sides throw games away constantly. Create counterplay (a passed pawn, a threat, an attack), trade his attacking pieces, and keep the position complicated.

**After the game:** log your 3 worst moves and one lesson (the dashboard's Blunder Report does this in 30 seconds). 10 minutes away from the board, then prepare for the next round like it is the first. **Never tilt — one loss is 1 round; a tilt spiral costs 3.**

## 7.2 Tournament math (how to actually win the event)
- 5 rounds: **3.5/5 usually wins a district event.** Draw with the strongest players, take full points from everyone else. You do not need to beat everyone.
- **vs a stronger player:** trade into an equal endgame, keep pieces active, never take unnecessary risks. They will over-push and blunder — they always do at club level.
- **vs a weaker player:** no tricks, no early queen attacks. Develop, castle, take the center, and wait. They will lose material by move 15.
- **vs the trap player:** you know all 10 traps now. Refuse the bait, develop, and he has nothing.

## 7.3 The 10-minute review ritual (this is how you get better *during* the tournament)
1. Open the Blunder Report — find your 3 worst moves.
2. For each: **what did I miss?** (a check, a capture, his threat, a hanging piece, a tactic)
3. Write the lesson in ONE line. Keep a running list; read it before the next round.
4. Check the opening: at move 10, was I developed and castled?
5. Check the endgame: did I know my plan? Did I trade into something I could convert?
6. Then let it go.
